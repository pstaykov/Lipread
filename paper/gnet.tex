\noindent\rule{\textwidth}{1pt}
\begin{abstract}
\noindent
We present GNet, a visual lip-reading model for German word-level classification trained on GLips, a corpus of approximately 250{,}000 clips of Hessian Parliament speakers covering 500 word classes. GNet couples a 3D-CNN and ResNet-18 visual frontend with a lightweight temporal back-end consisting of a multi-scale depthwise convolutional stem followed by a two-layer Transformer encoder. It is compared against an MS-TCN back-end sharing the frontend, the data pipeline and the training recipe, so that the comparison isolates the temporal model. On the 15-word GLips benchmark GNet reaches $59.3\%$ top-1 accuracy from scratch and $69.5\%$ with in-domain GLips-500 to GLips-15 transfer, compared with the previously published $48.4\%$ \citep{ameer2024} and $50.9\%$ from scratch or $54.8\%$ with cross-lingual LRW pretraining \citep{schwiebert2022glips}. We also report the first results on the full GLips vocabulary ($498$ classes with a complete published split), reaching $33.5\%$ top-1 accuracy against a chance rate of $0.2\%$. Controlled ablations indicate that the improvement is attributable primarily to preprocessing and training data rather than to the temporal architecture: mouth-ROI preprocessing accounts for $29.2$ points of top-1 accuracy, whereas the Transformer and MS-TCN back-ends are statistically indistinguishable on the 15-word task ($59.3$ against $59.2$ from scratch). The Transformer's advantage appears on the $498$-class task, where it gains $2.4$ points ($5.6\sigma$ over $24{,}900$ test clips) using $2.9\times$ fewer temporal parameters. Negative results are reported in full, including an attentive-pooling head that does not outperform mean pooling.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Visual speech recognition (VSR), the classification of spoken words from silent video of a speaker's face, has advanced rapidly on English benchmarks such as LRW, but progress in other languages remains limited by data availability. For German the only word-level benchmark is GLips \citep{schwiebert2022glips}, and published results on it cover a 15-word subset only: $50.9\%$ top-1 accuracy training from scratch, and $54.8\%$ when transferring from the larger English LRW corpus. No result has been reported for the full 500-word vocabulary, and no study has isolated the contribution of the temporal back-end under a fixed frontend and training recipe.

This paper addresses both gaps. We build GNet, a lip-reading model whose visual frontend follows the standard design for word-level VSR (a single spatiotemporal convolution followed by a per-frame ResNet-18) and whose temporal back-end is a multi-scale depthwise convolutional stem feeding a shallow Transformer encoder. Holding the frontend, the data pipeline, the augmentation stack and the optimiser settings fixed, we substitute only the temporal module for an MS-TCN baseline, so that reported accuracy differences are attributable to the back-end. We evaluate the same matched pair across three settings: the full vocabulary, the 15-word subset from scratch, and GLips-500 to GLips-15 in-domain transfer, with architecture and preprocessing ablations in addition.

GNet exceeds the published results on this benchmark by a substantial margin. Our ablations attribute the gain primarily to preprocessing and training data rather than to the temporal architecture. On the 15-word task the two back-ends differ by $0.13$ points ($0.05\sigma$), whereas removing the mouth-ROI crop costs $29.2$ points, an effect an order of magnitude larger than any back-end difference we measured. The Transformer's advantage is confined to the full-vocabulary task, where $24{,}900$ test clips provide sufficient statistical resolution ($+2.4$ points, $5.6\sigma$), and it is obtained with a $2.9\times$ smaller temporal module. We therefore characterise it as a parameter-efficiency result with a large-vocabulary accuracy benefit rather than as a general accuracy advantage. Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{New best published results on the GLips 15-word benchmark.} GNet reaches $59.3\%$ top-1 accuracy from scratch, exceeding the $48.4\%$ of \citet{ameer2024} by $10.9$ points and the $50.9\%$ from-scratch baseline of \citet{schwiebert2022glips} by $8.4$ points, on the same corpus, the same published split and, for the former, the same word set. With in-domain transfer this rises to $69.5\%$, and to $70.3\%$ for a two-back-end ensemble.
    \item \textbf{First full-vocabulary GLips benchmark.} We report the first results on the complete GLips vocabulary ($498$ of the $500$ classes have a usable published split), reaching $33.5\%$ top-1 and $53.4\%$ top-5 accuracy against a chance rate of $0.2\%$, with a matched MS-TCN baseline.
    \item \textbf{In-domain transfer as an alternative to cross-lingual transfer.} Pretraining on the full GLips vocabulary and fine-tuning on the 15-word subset adds $10.1$ points over training the same model from scratch, and exceeds the published LRW to GLips cross-lingual result by $14.7$ points. The two transfer settings are not directly comparable, and we treat this as evidence that in-domain pretraining is a viable option for low-resource VSR rather than as a like-for-like comparison.
    \item \textbf{A controlled MS-TCN and Transformer ablation.} Both back-ends share the frontend, data, augmentation and optimiser. The Transformer gains $2.4$ points on the full vocabulary ($5.6\sigma$) and ties on the 15-word task ($0.05\sigma$), using $2.9\times$ fewer temporal parameters ($2.2$M against $6.4$M) and $1.3\times$ fewer overall ($13.4$M against $17.6$M).
    \item \textbf{An accounting of the sources of the improvement, including negative results.} Ablations isolate mouth-ROI preprocessing ($29.2$ points), the convolutional stem ($2.4$ points) and the attentive-pooling head ($-1.2$ points, indicating no gain over mean pooling).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Architectures for large-vocabulary visual speech recognition}

GNet's frontend and one of its two temporal back-ends derive from the LRW and LRW-1000 literature. \citet{stafylakis2017combining} established the standard frontend: a single spatiotemporal convolution over short frame windows, followed by a per-frame 2D ResNet and a recurrent temporal model. \citet{martinez2020lipreading} replaced the recurrent back-end with a multi-scale temporal convolutional network (MS-TCN), simplifying training to a single stage, improving generalisation across sequence lengths, and setting state-of-the-art results on LRW and LRW-1000. This 3D-CNN, ResNet-18 and MS-TCN combination is the direct ancestor of both of GNet's configurations. Later work extended the convolutional line: the densely connected TCN of \citet{ma2021densely} added skip connections between blocks to widen the temporal receptive field, and more recent multi-dilated variants match or exceed TCN accuracy at lower parameter cost \citep{lee2025td3net}. Attention- and Transformer-based back-ends form a separate line, replacing the recurrent or convolutional temporal module outright \citep{prajwal2022subword}.

GNet combines both approaches: we retain a multi-scale convolutional stem and place a shallow Transformer encoder on top of it, reporting both variants as a matched pair rather than as a single substitution. To our knowledge none of the cited work performs a controlled MS-TCN and Transformer comparison holding data, frontend and training recipe fixed; architectural changes there are evaluated against a fixed prior baseline, which confounds the back-end's contribution with concurrent changes in the training procedure. Our results suggest this confound is material, since under a matched recipe the back-end gap is small and does not consistently favour one family.

\subsection{German visual speech recognition}

\citet{schwiebert2022glips} introduced GLips, approximately $250{,}000$ clips of Hessian Parliament speakers over $500$ word classes, formatted to match LRW so that cross-lingual transfer is possible, and showed that LRW to GLips transfer improves both convergence speed and final accuracy relative to training from scratch. This established the first German VSR baseline, but on a 15-class subset only, via cross-lingual transfer only, and without a full-vocabulary result. The reported figures are $50.9\%$ top-1 accuracy from scratch and $54.8\%$ with LRW pretraining.

\citet{ameer2024} benchmark a NASNetMobile-based pipeline on the same 15-class GLips subset, running a 16-configuration sweep crossing four pretrained 2D backbones with four temporal back-ends, and comparing against two further structured-dataset baselines. Their best configuration (NASNetMobile with a CNN back-end, $4.68$M parameters) reaches $48.40\%$ accuracy and $0.485$ macro-$F_1$ without in-domain transfer.

The two pipelines differ substantially in preprocessing. \citet{ameer2024} avoid face detection, processing uncropped frames at $128\times128$ with image- and feature-level augmentation, on the grounds that cropping discards information relevant to unconstrained data. GNet uses MediaPipe FaceMesh \citep{lugaresi2019mediapipe} to extract a tracked, scale-normalised mouth crop per frame (Section~\ref{sec:pre}). Section~\ref{sec:results} quantifies the effect of this choice, which is the largest single factor in our results, and Section~\ref{sec:ameer} reports the behaviour of our architecture under their input regime.

\subsection{Summary of the gap addressed}

No prior study on GLips, including the dataset paper, the sweep of \citet{ameer2024}, or the structured-dataset baselines they cite, evaluates the full vocabulary, runs a matched MS-TCN and Transformer ablation, or compares in-domain transfer (GLips-500 to GLips-15) against the cross-lingual LRW transfer that has so far been the only transfer result reported for this corpus. GNet is narrower than \citet{ameer2024} in backbone diversity and relies on landmark-tracked mouth-ROI cropping rather than their detection-free design. To our knowledge it is nonetheless the first work to vary vocabulary size, transfer setting and back-end architecture together in a controlled comparison on GLips.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:arch}

We define the visual encoder once and reuse it unchanged for both temporal back-ends, so that the comparison isolates the temporal model.

\paragraph{Shared visual encoder.}
An input clip $x \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times T \times H \times W}$ is processed by a \emph{Frontend3D}, a single 3D convolution with kernel $k=(5,7,7)$ and stride $(1,2,2)$ that preserves the temporal axis while halving the spatial axes, followed by batch normalisation, ReLU and a spatial max-pool. Each of the $T$ frames is then passed independently through the ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18 stages (\texttt{layer1} to \texttt{layer4}), yielding a per-frame feature $f_t \in \mathbb{R}^{512}$ after global spatial average pooling. GLips clips are $1.16\,\mathrm{s}$ at $25\,\mathrm{fps}$; we sample $T = 25$ frames per clip at $H = W = 88$. Both back-ends consume the same feature sequence $F \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times 512}$, and the encoder accounts for $11.2$M of each model's parameters.

\paragraph{Temporal back-end (GNet).}
$F$ passes through (i) a linear projection $\mathrm{Linear}(512 \to d) + \mathrm{LayerNorm}$ with $d = 256$; (ii) a multi-scale convolutional stem of two \texttt{MSTemporalBlock}s, each averaging three parallel depthwise branches with kernels $k \in \{3,5,7\}$, where each branch comprises a depthwise convolution, a pointwise convolution, batch normalisation and GELU, under a residual connection and LayerNorm; (iii) a learned positional embedding; (iv) a $2$-layer pre-norm Transformer encoder ($8$ heads, FFN width $1024$, GELU, dropout $0.2$); and (v) attentive temporal pooling followed by dropout and a linear classifier. Attentive pooling scores each token with a small two-layer network $g$, forms weights $w_t = \mathrm{softmax}\big(g(z_t)\big)$, and returns $\sum_t w_t\, z_t$. The temporal module totals $2.2$M parameters, giving $13.4$M for the whole model on the 15-class task.

\paragraph{Temporal back-end (MS-TCN baseline).}
The same $F$ is fed to a multi-scale temporal convolutional network operating directly on the $512$-d sequence. It stacks $B = 4$ MS-TCN blocks of width $384$; each block runs parallel 1-D temporal convolutions with kernels $k \in \{3,5,7\}$ at increasing dilation, followed by batch normalisation, ReLU and a residual connection. A final temporal average pool over $T$ feeds a linear classifier. With no attention but wider channels, the baseline's temporal module uses $6.4$M parameters against $2.2$M for GNet, and $17.6$M against $13.4$M overall.

\subsection{Design rationale}

Because both back-ends share the encoder, any accuracy gap is attributable to the temporal model. The frontend is the standard design for word-level VSR, a single spatiotemporal convolution followed by a per-frame ResNet, as introduced by \citet{stafylakis2017combining} and reused by subsequent LRW systems \citep{martinez2020lipreading, ma2021densely}, so reusing it unchanged is what makes the comparison an ablation of the back-end.

\emph{Temporal stride $1$ in the Frontend3D.} The frontend convolves over short windows (kernel $k=(5,7,7)$) at unit temporal stride, so that no frames are merged before the back-end, preserving the short-range articulatory dynamics that separate visually similar words. The same short-window, unit-stride design underlies the canonical LRW frontend \citep{stafylakis2017combining} and the sub-clip encoders used in more recent attention-based systems \citep{prajwal2022subword}.

\emph{Multi-scale depthwise branches ($k\in\{3,5,7\}$).} Multi-scale temporal convolution is the basis of the TCN back-end's competitiveness: \citet{martinez2020lipreading} improved over the recurrent baseline by mixing short and long temporal receptive fields, and dense or dilated successors \citep{ma2021densely, lee2025td3net} indicate that broad receptive-field coverage, rather than depth, is the principal accuracy lever, and that it can be obtained at low cost. Our depthwise branches are an inexpensive realisation of this principle; Section~\ref{sec:results} measures their contribution at $2.4$ points of top-1 accuracy.

\emph{Shallow ($2$-layer) Transformer.} GLips provides $500$ clips per class \citep{schwiebert2022glips}, that is, $400$ training clips per word under the published split, a regime in which self-attention overfits readily. We cap the stack at $2$ layers, reduced from an initial $4$ which overfit measurably, and rely on dropout.

\emph{Attentive pooling.} Each clip is largely non-articulatory, since the target word occupies only a fraction of the $25$ frames, so a uniform temporal average dilutes the informative frames, and query-based attentive pooling is intended to up-weight them \citep{prajwal2022subword}. The ablation in Section~\ref{sec:results} does not support this rationale: a mean-pooling variant performs equivalently. We retain attentive pooling as the default configuration because the transfer runs were trained with it.

\paragraph{Transfer procedure.}
\label{sec:transfer}
GLips is designed to be LRW-compatible, and \citet{schwiebert2022glips} show that transfer between the two corpora accelerates convergence and improves accuracy. We apply the same mechanism within GLips. For both back-ends, transfer warm-starts every tensor of the feature extractor whose name and shape match, comprising the 3D frontend, the ResNet, the temporal module, the positional embedding and the pooling head, and reinitialises only the classifier, so that a model with a different class count warm-starts cleanly (\texttt{load\_transfer\_weights}). The source is always the corresponding full-vocabulary model of the same back-end family, so the transfer comparison remains matched.

\section{Experimental Setup}

\subsection{Dataset, splits and preprocessing}
\label{sec:pre}

GLips \citep{schwiebert2022glips} ships with a fixed per-class partition into \emph{train}, \emph{val} and \emph{test} folders holding $400$, $50$ and $50$ clips per word class respectively. We train on \emph{train}, select checkpoints on \emph{val}, and report all headline numbers on the held-out \emph{test} folder. We use the published folder split verbatim, with no source-disjoint re-partitioning, so that our numbers are comparable to prior work adopting the same split. On the 15-class benchmark this yields $6{,}000$ training, $750$ validation and $750$ test clips. For the full-vocabulary task we use the $498$ classes that have a complete published split; two classes (\emph{soll}, \emph{hier}) are degenerate in the corpus, having no validation folder, and are dropped so that train and validation cover an identical class set. This gives approximately $199{,}000$ training and $24{,}900$ test clips.

For the 15-word subset we adopt the word set of \citet{ameer2024}: \emph{aber, aufgaben, bleibt, darüber, digitalisierung, einmal, finden, gegen, geworden, herrn, investitionen, kommunen, linke, möglichkeit, passiert}. We do not use the first fifteen words alphabetically, which are dominated by short, visually similar function words and cap out near $28\%$ accuracy in our own runs. A randomly drawn subset would make our results incomparable to prior work, since accuracy depends strongly on which words are drawn, and exhaustive evaluation is infeasible, there being $\binom{500}{15} \approx 1.88 \times 10^{28}$ possible draws. \citet{schwiebert2022glips} do not report their 15-word draw, so our comparison against their figures is approximate; the comparison against \citet{ameer2024} is on their published word set.

\paragraph{Mouth-ROI pipeline.}
\label{sec:roi}
For every frame, MediaPipe FaceMesh \citep{lugaresi2019mediapipe} localises the lip landmarks. An axis-aligned square box is fitted around the lip region with side length $s = \texttt{box\_scale} \times \max(w_\text{mouth}, h_\text{mouth})$ at $\texttt{box\_scale}=1.6$, exponentially smoothed across frames ($\alpha=0.4$) and held over brief detection dropouts. The box is cropped from the native $256\times256$ frame and resized to $96\times96$, and the original audio is re-muxed so that multimodal variants retain sound. At training time the $96\times96$ crop is randomly cropped to $88\times88$ (centre crop at evaluation), $25$ frames are temporally sampled per clip, and the augmentation stack of Section~\ref{sec:aug} is applied with ImageNet channel normalisation.

\paragraph{Baseline-matched pipeline.}
To separate preprocessing from architecture we also reproduce the input recipe of \citet{ameer2024} on the uncropped frames: no face detection or mouth-ROI crop, each frame resized directly to $128\times128$ with no spatial crop, $16$ frames sampled per clip, per-clip min--max normalisation to $[0,1]$, and random horizontal flipping as the only augmentation. Holding the model and optimiser fixed while switching only between these two pipelines isolates the contribution of the mouth-ROI preprocessing from that of the architecture.

\subsection{Data augmentation}
\label{sec:aug}

Lip reading depends on the temporal evolution of mouth shape, so our augmentation is built around one invariant: every stochastic transform parameter is drawn once per clip and applied identically to all $T$ frames. A spatial jitter, colour shift or occlusion varying from frame to frame would inject apparent motion unrelated to articulation. All augmentation is active during training only; at validation and test the transform reduces to a deterministic resize, centre crop and normalisation.

\emph{Geometric.} A single affine warp per clip, with rotation drawn uniformly from $[-10^\circ,10^\circ]$, isotropic scale from $[0.9,1.1]$, and translation up to $6\%$ of the frame along each axis, followed by a random $88\times88$ crop (centre crop at evaluation) and a horizontal flip with probability $0.5$. The affine is applied before the crop so that the crop discards most of the border region the warp introduces.

\emph{Photometric.} Per-clip brightness and contrast jitter, each a multiplicative factor in $[0.8,1.2]$, and random conversion of the whole clip to grayscale with probability $0.2$. Grayscaling removes a skin-tone and lighting shortcut: because lip reading depends on shape rather than colour, removing the colour channel on a fifth of the clips discourages memorisation of speaker- and studio-specific appearance.

\emph{Temporal.} Two mechanisms perturb the time axis while holding the frame count fixed at $25$. A speed perturbation resamples the $25$ frames from a randomly warped and offset sub-window of the clip, corresponding to an effective speaking-rate factor in $[0.8,1.2]$, which augments articulation speed and adds a temporal crop. Temporal masking then zeros between one and two contiguous spans of up to $4$ frames each, encouraging the temporal back-end to integrate over the whole utterance rather than rely on a few decisive frames.

\emph{Occlusion.} With probability $0.25$ we apply random erasing: a single rectangle covering $2$ to $20\%$ of the frame area at aspect ratio in $[0.3,3.3]$ is filled with a flat value. The same rectangle is erased in every frame, consistent with the clip-invariance principle above, simulating partial static occlusion of the mouth.

\subsection{Training setup}
\label{sec:train}

All models are optimised with AdamW (weight decay $0.05$) under a differential learning rate: the ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18 backbone is fine-tuned at $\eta_\text{bb}=10^{-4}$, while all freshly initialised parameters (the 3D stem, the temporal module, the positional embedding, the pooling head and the classifier) are trained at $\eta_\text{head}=10^{-3}$. The schedule is a $3$-epoch linear warm-up from $0.1\,\eta$ to $\eta$ followed by cosine annealing. We use batch size $32$, gradient-norm clipping at $1.0$, and bfloat16 automatic mixed precision, falling back to fp16 with loss scaling where bf16 is unavailable. Every configuration in a given comparison uses identical settings, with the component under test as the only variable.

The 15-class runs use the full regularisation recipe: label smoothing ($\varepsilon=0.1$), batch-level Mixup and CutMix, the augmentation stack of Section~\ref{sec:aug}, and an exponential moving average (EMA) of the weights with decay $0.999$. All validation and checkpoint selection use the EMA weights, and the reported checkpoint is the EMA snapshot at peak validation top-1 accuracy. These runs are budgeted at $80$ epochs with early stopping on validation top-1 accuracy. The full-vocabulary runs, which serve as transfer feature sources (Section~\ref{sec:transfer}) and whose classifiers are subsequently discarded, use a plain recipe without EMA or Mixup and are trained for $30$ epochs, which is sufficient for the frontend and temporal back-end to converge as a feature extractor and keeps full-vocabulary training tractable on our hardware. All experiments run on a single NVIDIA RTX~3060 ($12$\,GB).

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

We report top-$1$ and top-$5$ classification accuracy and macro-averaged $F_1$. For $N$ clips with predicted logits $\hat{\mathbf{y}}_i$ and ground-truth label $y_i$, top-$k$ accuracy is
\[
\text{Top-}k = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\mathbf{1}\!\left[\, y_i \in \operatorname{top\text{-}}k\big(\hat{\mathbf{y}}_i\big) \right],
\]
where $\operatorname{top\text{-}}k(\cdot)$ returns the indices of the $k$ largest logits, with $k=\min(5,C)$ for a $C$-class task. Top-$1$ is the primary metric for model selection and all head-to-head comparisons; top-$5$ characterises near-miss behaviour on a visually confusable vocabulary; macro-$F_1$ is reported because it is the secondary metric used by \citet{ameer2024} and because it is insensitive to the mild class-count imbalance introduced by dropping degenerate classes. Every row of every results table is scored by a single shared evaluation script under one protocol, with the same split, class list and batching, applying each row's own preprocessing.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

All numbers in this section are top-1 and top-5 accuracy and macro-$F_1$ on the held-out GLips \emph{test} folder. Where we compare two of our own configurations we also quote the significance of the gap, computed as $|\Delta| / \sqrt{\mathrm{SE}_a^2 + \mathrm{SE}_b^2}$ with $\mathrm{SE} = \sqrt{p(1-p)/N}$. This is relevant here because the 15-word test split contains only $N = 750$ clips, so differences below approximately $5$ points are not resolvable on it. The full-vocabulary split, at $N = 24{,}900$, resolves differences approximately six times finer.

Table~\ref{tab:main} gives the main comparison, from which three observations follow.

\emph{On the full vocabulary the Transformer back-end is ahead.} GNet reaches $33.53\%$ top-1 accuracy against MS-TCN's $31.18\%$, a gap of $2.35$ points at $5.6\sigma$ over $24{,}900$ test clips, with a corresponding $2.7$-point advantage in macro-$F_1$, and obtains it with $4.2$M fewer parameters. This is the only architectural difference in our study that lies clearly outside the noise floor. Both figures are, to our knowledge, the first reported for the full GLips vocabulary; against a chance rate of $0.2\%$, $33.5\%$ top-1 and $53.4\%$ top-5 accuracy indicate that the task is learnable but far from saturated.

\emph{On the 15-word task the two back-ends are indistinguishable.} From scratch they differ by $0.13$ points ($0.05\sigma$), and under transfer by $0.94$ points ($0.39\sigma$). Neither gap is resolvable at $N=750$, and the top-5 column favours MS-TCN in both rows. We therefore do not claim a Transformer accuracy advantage at this vocabulary size. GNet does, however, reach the same accuracy with a $2.9\times$ smaller temporal module.

\emph{In-domain transfer contributes more than the back-end choice.} Warm-starting from the full-vocabulary model adds $10.1$ points for GNet ($59.33$ to $69.47$) and $9.3$ points for MS-TCN ($59.20$ to $68.53$), an effect an order of magnitude larger than any architectural difference at this scale. A softmax-average ensemble of the two transfer models adds a further $0.8$ points, reaching $70.27\%$; we report it for completeness, noting that it doubles inference cost for a sub-$\sigma$ gain.

Adding in-domain GLips-498 pretraining, which uses only GLips training data but more of it than the 15-class training set alone, raises this to $69.47\%$, exceeding the $54.8\%$ that \citet{schwiebert2022glips} obtain by transferring from the larger English LRW corpus.

Two qualifications apply. First, \citet{schwiebert2022glips} do not report which fifteen words they used, and word-subset difficulty varies considerably, since the first fifteen words alphabetically cap out near $28\%$ in our own runs, so the comparison against their figures is approximate in a way that the comparison against \citet{ameer2024} is not. Second, in-domain and cross-lingual transfer are not equivalent settings: our source and target share recording conditions, speakers and language, whereas LRW to GLips transfer must additionally bridge a cross-lingual and cross-corpus gap. The $14.7$-point margin is therefore evidence that in-domain pretraining is a viable alternative for low-resource VSR rather than a like-for-like comparison against cross-lingual transfer.

\begin{figure}[t]

\emph{Mouth-ROI preprocessing is the dominant factor.} Training the identical model on uncropped full-face frames reduces top-1 accuracy from $59.33\%$ to $30.13\%$, a loss of $29.2$ points at $11.9\sigma$, and the only effect in our study larger than a few points. It is approximately $12\times$ the size of the largest back-end difference we measured. This indicates that landmark-tracked mouth cropping contributes more on GLips than any temporal-architecture decision evaluated here, and that a corpus of $500$ clips per class does not provide enough data for the model to learn to attend to the mouth region without an explicit cropping stage.

\emph{The convolutional stem contributes modestly.} Removing it and feeding per-frame features directly into the Transformer costs $2.4$ points. The direction is consistent with the multi-scale receptive-field argument of \citet{martinez2020lipreading} and we retain the stem, but at $0.9\sigma$ on this split the effect is not established and would require additional seeds or the full-vocabulary split to confirm.

\emph{Attentive pooling does not outperform mean pooling.} A plain mean-pooling variant scores $1.2$ points higher in top-1 accuracy and $1.8$ points higher in macro-$F_1$. The difference is not significant ($0.5\sigma$), so the two are best regarded as equivalent, but the design rationale in Section~\ref{sec:arch}, that attentive pooling should up-weight the articulated frames in an otherwise silent clip, is not supported. One possible explanation is that the augmentation stack already enforces temporal integration, since random temporal masking zeroes short spans specifically to prevent the model from relying on a few decisive frames.

\subsection{Per-class behaviour}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_confusion.pdf}
\caption{Row-normalised confusion matrix for GNet with in-domain transfer on the GLips-15 test split. Cells give the rate per true label, so the diagonal is per-class recall. Recall is highest for long, morphologically distinctive words and lowest for short function words, which also account for the largest off-diagonal masses.}
\label{fig:confusion}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:confusion} gives the row-normalised confusion matrix for the best single model. Per-class recall varies widely, from $92\%$ for \emph{digitalisierung} and $90\%$ for \emph{aufgaben}, \emph{investitionen} and \emph{kommunen}, down to $30\%$ for \emph{herrn} and $42\%$ for \emph{einmal}. Recall correlates strongly with orthographic word length (Pearson $r = 0.74$): classes of at least nine characters average $88.0\%$ recall, while those of six characters or fewer average $53.7\%$. The largest individual confusions are \emph{linke} predicted as \emph{gegen} ($18\%$), and \emph{herrn} and \emph{aber} both predicted as \emph{einmal} ($14\%$ each). This is consistent with the visual-phonetic structure of the task rather than with a defect of the model: short words span fewer frames and offer fewer distinguishing visemes, and several of the low-recall classes are near-homophenes that are not separable from the lip channel alone. It also indicates that aggregate accuracy on a 15-word subset depends substantially on the length distribution of the words drawn, which is a further reason that comparisons across different word draws, such as the one against \citet{schwiebert2022glips}, should be treated as approximate.

\subsection{Behaviour under the baseline input regime}
\label{sec:ameer}

To bound how much of the improvement is architectural, we also trained both back-ends under the input regime of \citet{ameer2024}: uncropped $128\times128$ frames, $16$ frames per clip, min--max normalisation and horizontal flipping as the only augmentation. GNet reaches $16.53\%$ top-1 accuracy and MS-TCN $20.93\%$, both well below their published $48.40\%$.

This experiment characterises our architecture under their input regime and is not a reproduction of their method, so it should not be read as a head-to-head comparison. The two designs differ in how they use the frontend. Theirs is a $4.68$M-parameter ImageNet-pretrained 2D CNN used as a feature extractor, with augmentation applied at the feature level and a light temporal head trained on top. Ours is a $13.4$M-parameter network with a 3D convolutional stem, trained end-to-end with the ResNet backbone fine-tuned. Without the mouth-ROI crop and without clip-level augmentation beyond flipping, our model overfits severely, reaching $75\%$ training accuracy against $17\%$ on validation, which is the expected failure mode for an end-to-end model of this capacity on $6{,}000$ clips of uncropped faces. The result is consistent with the ablation above: GNet's accuracy is contingent on the ROI crop and the augmentation stack.

\section{Discussion and limitations}

\emph{The improvement is data-driven rather than architectural.} GNet's $10.9$-point margin over \citet{ameer2024} is a benchmark improvement on a shared split and word set, but our ablations attribute it primarily to preprocessing and augmentation rather than to the temporal back-end. A $29$-point preprocessing effect that exceeds every architectural difference measured here suggests that reported architectural gains on small VSR corpora are difficult to interpret unless preprocessing is held fixed across the compared systems.

\emph{The Transformer's benefit is limited in scope.} We observe it only at $498$ classes. This is consistent with the expectation that self-attention and long-range temporal context become useful as the label space grows and finer temporal discrimination is required, whereas at $15$ classes a multi-scale convolutional back-end already captures what the frontend provides. On accuracy grounds we do not find a basis for preferring the Transformer back-end over MS-TCN for small-vocabulary tasks; on parameter-efficiency grounds it is preferable at any size, and on both grounds for large vocabularies.

\emph{Statistical power is the principal methodological limitation.} The published 15-word test split contains $750$ clips, which resolves differences of approximately $5$ points. Several ablations, and both 15-word back-end comparisons, fall below that threshold and are marked as such. All results are single-seed; a multi-seed protocol with confidence intervals would be required to settle the sub-$\sigma$ comparisons, and was outside the compute budget available on a single RTX~3060.

\emph{Further limitations.} We use the published split, which is not speaker-disjoint, so our numbers, like those of \citet{ameer2024} and \citet{schwiebert2022glips} on the same split, include speaker overlap between train and test and should be read as within-corpus rather than speaker-independent performance. GLips consists of parliamentary footage: front-facing, well-lit, adult and broadcast-quality. Our reliance on mouth-ROI cropping suits that setting and may transfer poorly to unconstrained video where face detection fails, which is the concern \citet{ameer2024} raise for their detection-free design, and Section~\ref{sec:ameer} provides no evidence against it. Finally, the full-vocabulary models were trained for $30$ epochs under a plain recipe, since they serve as transfer sources, so the $33.5\%$ figure should be read as an initial baseline rather than a ceiling.

\section{Conclusion}

GNet is a German visual lip-reading system that improves on the published results for the GLips 15-word benchmark, reaching $59.3\%$ top-1 accuracy from scratch against a previous best of $50.9\%$ from scratch and $48.4\%$ for the most recent published pipeline, rising to $69.5\%$ with in-domain GLips-498 transfer and $70.3\%$ with a two-back-end ensemble, where the previous best transfer result was $54.8\%$ using cross-lingual LRW pretraining. We additionally report the first results on the full GLips vocabulary, at $33.5\%$ top-1 and $53.4\%$ top-5 accuracy across $498$ classes against a chance rate of $0.2\%$.

Controlled ablations attribute the improvement primarily to data and preprocessing rather than to the temporal architecture, with $29.2$ points from mouth-ROI preprocessing and $10.1$ points from in-domain transfer, while the Transformer and MS-TCN back-ends are statistically indistinguishable at this vocabulary size. For the temporal back-end, the Transformer is the preferable default: it is ahead on the full $498$-class vocabulary by $2.4$ points ($5.6\sigma$) while using $2.9\times$ fewer temporal parameters, and ties elsewhere. This constitutes a parameter-efficiency result with a large-vocabulary accuracy benefit rather than a general accuracy advantage. We also report that the attentive-pooling head, adopted on the reasoning that most frames in a clip are non-articulatory, does not outperform mean pooling.

The direction these results suggest is not a different temporal module but more data and better-targeted input: a speaker-disjoint German benchmark large enough to resolve the differences this one cannot, and self-supervised audio-visual pretraining on German at a scale where the frontend can learn to localise the mouth without an explicit cropping stage.
